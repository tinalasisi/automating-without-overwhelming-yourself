Speaker notes

About 1 minute. ROI for automation: the time you spend building should be less than the time you save over a reasonable period.

The confession: I build Rube Goldberg machines. This triggers that, that feeds into this other thing, it is all so elegant. Six months later something changes, the whole thing breaks, and I have no idea why. So pausing to ask “is this worth it?” comes from experience.

Hidden costs

2 min

Some costs a calculator can’t see.

Maintenance

External systems change all the time.

  • An update happens.
  • A feature gets renamed.
  • A service changes how it connects.

Over-engineering

Aim for “good enough” to avoid feature creep.

  • Features you don’t need.
  • More complex than it has to be.
  • “While I’m at it, I might as well also…”

Ask first: how would I know if this broke?

7 of 11 client proposals raise cost and upkeep. Your clients already know this.
Speaker notes

About 2 minutes. Maintenance: automations break, and a silent failure is worse than a visible error. Every automated process you create is a system you are now responsible for keeping alive.

Over-engineering: “while I’m at it, I might as well also…” is feature creep, the enemy of “good enough.”

The upkeep worries in the client proposals: who maintains it, ongoing tool costs, energy use.

The value of manual processes

2 min

Some costs aren’t about time at all. Guess what each one would cost you.

The hand-typed weekly report

Looks like a perfect automation candidate.

What would you lose? Awareness

Putting it together may be how you stay informed.

Personal welcome emails

Easy to template and send.

What would you lose? Connection

Writing them may be how you build relationships with new people.

Reviewing data by hand

Slow and repetitive.

What would you lose? Quality

It may be how you catch errors before they snowball.

Automation handles rules well. It handles nuance poorly. Automating judgment often means automating errors.

Speaker notes

About 2 minutes. Let the room guess before you open each card.

Automating these might save you twenty minutes, but it could cost you awareness, relationships, or quality.

The “describe it to a robot” test: if you can write the exact steps with no room for interpretation, you can probably automate it. If you keep adding “unless…” and “it depends…”, you are in judgment territory.

Every project today has a “stays manual” line. This is where it comes from.

Solving the wrong problem

2 min

Consider that the current system may be fine, with no real problem to solve!

  • Sometimes the task shouldn’t exist at all.
  • Maybe you don’t need an automated report. Maybe you need a five-minute conversation with your team.
  • Is it slow because of my time, or because I’m waiting on approvals or on someone else? Automation won’t fix the second one.
8 of 11 client proposals say they are open to simpler or non-AI approaches. Your clients left the door open.

Automation decisions require judgment, not just math.

The chart can tell you whether the numbers work out. It can’t tell you whether you should do it.

Speaker notes

About 2 minutes. Only you can decide, by thinking about context, about what you would lose, and about whether the task is actually the problem you need to solve.

Today’s thesis: the smallest useful unit

3 min

The IDEA framework from my course:

  1. I

    Identify

    What does “done” look like?

  2. D

    Document

    Data source, transformation, output. What’s fixed? What varies?

  3. Today’s focus E

    Experiment

    Choose one component to test. Start with the smallest workable version.

  4. A

    Adjust

    What could break if something changes? Who else is affected?

Today we apply that to each client project.

Find the smallest useful step worth automating. Have AI build it. Run it on test data. Keep the judgment calls manual.
