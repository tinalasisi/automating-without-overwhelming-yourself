Keep it alive

2 min

How would you know it broke?

From my course: “External systems change all the time.” An update ships, a feature gets renamed, a connection changes. Your automation stops.

Every client project has the same five parts

  1. Trigger
  2. Extraction
  3. Decision (rule or manual)
  4. Write-back
  5. Log

The last one tells you it broke.

Silent failures

No error. It just stops.

No new rows, and nobody notices. Build one check that notices silence.

Zell’s next step has the same idea: a weekly “no movement in 14 days” alert.

A simple log

Every row gets a status and a timestamp.

“done” when it worked. “needs review” with a reason when it didn’t.

If the newest timestamp is old, something stopped.

Make it findable

2 min

Capturing information is automation.

You are automating your future self’s ability to find things.

1 · Easy enough that you’ll actually do it

Don’t: build templates, tagging systems and color codes.

The System Graveyard: the productivity apps you used enthusiastically for a week, then never opened again.

Do: a system you’ll use consistently, even if it’s imperfect. Quick capture, inside tools you already use.

2 · Add context to your notes

Avoid abbreviations. Write “conference”, not “conf.”

Write for someone else. In six months, that person is you.

Keyword vs. semantic search

Keyword search

Matches the exact words or phrases.

Semantic search

Matches meaning. A search for “Financial Forecast” also finds “Budget Planning” and “Resource Allocation”.

So you don’t have to guess your future keywords. Capture enough context that the meaning comes through.

Key takeaways

  1. Design for how you’ll actually search. Balance that against what’s easy to maintain.
  2. Don’t make an all-or-nothing switch. Start with one project.
  3. Use it for a week or two. Then honestly assess: am I actually using this?

Good enough and consistent beats perfect but complicated.

Before you automate the answer

3 min

Consider that the current system may be fine, with no real problem to solve.

Sometimes the task shouldn’t exist at all. Maybe you don’t need an automated report. Maybe you need a five-minute conversation with your team.

Automation decisions require judgment, not just math.
